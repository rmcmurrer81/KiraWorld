Jack Webb
I do not remember the exact Friday. It was sometime during the long stretch when I was homeless in Los Angeles and fighting to clear my name. Every court date seemed to lead to another one, and most days I was either looking for evidence, looking for somewhere to sleep, or trying to find something—anything—that would let me forget the case for a few hours.
Somewhere along the way I learned about a Friday-night Alcoholics Anonymous meeting held on the Universal Studios lot. People called it the Jack Webb meeting.
I was not really an alcoholic. Alcohol was never the reason I went. What caught my attention was Universal.
I had always loved movies and television, and here was a legitimate reason for an ordinary person to walk onto one of the most famous studio lots in Hollywood.
That Friday evening I walked up to the studio entrance—the gate I now believe was Gate 5 on Lankershim Boulevard. I expected questions. Maybe identification. Maybe someone would have to come out and meet me.
Instead, I approached the guard and said two words.
“Jack Webb.”
The guard seemed to know exactly what I meant.
He handed me an ordinary-looking paper pass.
That was it.
No complicated explanation. No secret handshake. No dramatic password like something out of a spy movie. Just Jack Webb and a plain piece of paper.
A few moments later I was walking inside Universal Studios.
For someone sleeping outside, there was something almost unreal about it. On the other side of the gate was Hollywood history—soundstages, offices and pieces of a world I had spent years trying to get close to. Outside the gates I worried about court, money, food and where I would sleep. Inside, for a little while, I could simply look around.
The meeting was connected with Building 2250, the Jack Webb Building. I still remember the number.
Inside was a large framed picture of Jack Webb.
I knew him from Dragnet, the stone-faced detective who wanted “just the facts.” There was something almost ironic about seeing his picture during that period of my life. Facts were exactly what I was desperately trying to get people to look at.
I attended the meeting. I listened.
But I would be lying if I said the meeting itself was the only reason I was there.
Part of me was there because the paper pass had opened a door that was normally closed. I could walk around Universal without sneaking in or pretending I belonged there. For a brief period, I actually did belong there—or at least that little piece of paper said I did.
When the meeting ended, I walked back out through the same gate.
The magic disappeared almost immediately.
I started the long walk through the streets toward Burbank. I remember passing through Toluca Lake at night, walking by the stores and restaurants along the way. Lights glowed through windows while people sat inside eating dinner or going about ordinary lives.
I kept walking.
Eventually I reached the area near Bob Hope Drive and the freeway ramp. I knew a place where I could get away from the sidewalk. I went between the bushes and a brick wall and climbed toward the freeway.
That was where I slept.
I found a place beside a large bush and lay down while cars rushed past above and beside me. They never really stopped. All night there was the sound of engines and tires against pavement.
A few hours earlier I had been inside Universal Studios, beneath a framed photograph of Jack Webb.
Now I was sleeping beside a freeway.
That was Los Angeles for me in those days.
I could walk a red carpet, appear in a movie, enter a famous studio or meet somebody I had watched on television—and then, when it was over, I still needed to figure out where I could safely close my eyes for the night.
The next morning the traffic was still there.
So was I.
